Cannabis withdrawal is real: the timeline nobody warns you about

What actually happens in your body and brain after you stop using cannabis, and why the hardest days often come later than you expect.

People get told marijuana isn't physically addictive and leave it at that. Then they quit after years of daily use and spend two weeks wondering why they feel like a different, worse person. The irritability, the sweating at night, the dreams that come back so hard they feel like a punishment — nobody warned them this was coming. So let's be specific about what actually happens and when.

The first week: your body recalibrates

THC is fat-soluble. It doesn't flush out in a day or two the way alcohol does. For heavy daily users, cannabinoids can linger in tissue for weeks, but the acute withdrawal window typically opens within 24 to 72 hours of your last use and peaks somewhere between days three and six.

What you might feel during that window: irritability that arrives without a reason, trouble falling asleep or staying asleep, night sweats, a low appetite, headaches, and a kind of restless physical discomfort that is hard to name. Some people describe it as being itchy on the inside. None of that is catastrophic, but it is real, and minimizing it doesn't help you plan for it.

Sleep is often the worst part. Cannabis suppresses REM sleep over time. When you stop, REM rebounds — sometimes dramatically. Vivid, emotionally intense dreams are extremely common and can last two to four weeks. Knowing that in advance doesn't make them pleasant, but it does make them less alarming.

The hardest part of cannabis withdrawal often isn't the first few days — it's the weeks after, when the physical symptoms are gone but the emotional ones are just getting started.

Weeks two through four: the emotional layer

This is the stretch most articles skip. The physical symptoms ease. You might think you're through it. Then comes a flat, gray feeling — low mood, low motivation, difficulty finding pleasure in things that used to work. Anxiety that seems to come from nowhere. A short fuse.

This happens because your endocannabinoid system, which is involved in mood regulation, stress response, and reward, has been outsourcing part of its job for however long you were using. It needs time to recalibrate. That window varies by how long and how heavily you used, but for many daily users it runs two to six weeks, sometimes longer.

This is also when cravings shift character. Early cravings are often physical — a habit loop your hands remember, a time of day your body associates with use. Later cravings tend to be emotional. You reach for the thing that used to make boredom, anxiety, or social discomfort manageable. That's not weakness. That's a gap where a coping mechanism used to be, and the work is finding what goes there instead.

What actually helps

Exercise is not a cliché here — it genuinely moves mood. Even a twenty-minute walk at the same time you used to use starts to interrupt the habit loop and gives your endocannabinoid system something to work with. It produces its own cannabinoids; that's not a metaphor.

Sleep hygiene matters more than it sounds. Consistent wake time, no screens in the dark, cool room. The rebound dreams are going to come regardless — the goal is to give yourself the best chance of actually staying asleep around them.

Write down what you're feeling, even roughly. Not because journaling is magic, but because unnamed feelings tend to grow. Putting a word to the gray doesn't fix it, but it creates a little distance from it.

The timeline nobody warns you about is survivable. It just goes longer than a week, and it asks more of you than white-knuckling the first three days. Now you know what's coming.
